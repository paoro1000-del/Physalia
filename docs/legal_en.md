# Japanese legal assessment: methods and unauthorized access

The Japanese and U.S. discussions are general interpretations, not guarantees for an individual deployment. Before deployment, consult a legal professional familiar with the applicable jurisdictions and the proposed configuration.

## U.S. legal assessment

Reviewed October 31, 2026. Creating and placing decoys in an environment owned or explicitly authorized by the operator ordinarily is not, by itself, unauthorized access to another party's system. The actual implementation and conduct matter, and other laws and contracts may still apply.

The federal [Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (18 U.S.C. § 1030)](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-118/t18/s1030/) addresses unauthorized or exceeding authorized access and other conduct under specific statutory requirements. Intruding into an apparent attacker's or a third party's system, or logging in with another person's credentials without authority, may raise CFAA issues even for defensive purposes. The [DOJ charging policy](https://www.justice.gov/jm/jm-9-48000-computer-fraud) is a departmental policy, not an exhaustive statement of the statute or civil liability.

Distinguish ordinary access logs from interception, storage and disclosure of communication content. Federal [18 U.S.C. § 2511](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-120/t18/s2511/) regulates certain interceptions and related conduct; its exceptions have specific conditions. Owning a system does not automatically permit unrestricted interception, and a single consent does not resolve every legal issue. State communications and privacy laws, rules concerning stored communications, sector-specific requirements, contracts and notice obligations may also apply. Review what is collected, locations of the parties, notice and consent, retention, access controls and disclosure to monitoring providers.

Consider third-party information and accidental messages for decoy databases and Data Dilution; agency impersonation and accidental access for Fake Trap; harm to third-party resources for Infinite Stream; and credential or communication-content collection for honeyword sessions. IP addresses and user agents can also raise privacy issues depending on handling. See the [FTC guide on protecting personal information](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/protecting-personal-information-guide-business) for data minimization, retention and access controls.

Reviewed October 30, 2026. This assessment concerns the supplied tools used in an environment the operator is authorized to manage. “Ordinarily not covered” is an assessment under Japan's Unauthorized Computer Access Act; it is not a blanket guarantee under all laws.

The Act prohibits, among other things, using another person's identification code without authority or bypassing access controls over a network to make restricted use possible (Articles 2(4) and 3). Acquisition, storage, provision and improper requests for identification codes have distinct requirements under Articles 4–7. Article 7 covers both making credential-request information publicly viewable and emailing it to an authorized user when the sender impersonates, or otherwise leads the user to believe they are, the access administrator. The statutory wording defines the conduct; its application depends on the presentation, recipients, parties and scope of consent in the specific case. Article 7 expressly excludes conduct performed with the consent of the relevant access administrator; this exception is limited to Article 7 and does not settle liability under other laws. The 2012 committee hearing identifies an awareness or training phishing site with site-administrator consent as an example. [e-Gov: the Act as of October 30, 2026](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/411AC0000000128?occasion_date=20261030) / [National Police Agency commentary](https://www.npa.go.jp/bureau/cyber/pdf/1_kaisetsu.pdf) / [House of Representatives: March 16, 2012 committee minutes](https://www.shugiin.go.jp/internet/itdb_kaigiroku.nsf/html/kaigiroku/000218020120316004.htm) (Japanese).

The National Police Agency's current illegal-information guidance also lists improper requests for identification codes under Article 7(1). [National Police Agency: Internet illegal and harmful information measures](https://www.npa.go.jp/bureau/cyber/countermeasures/illegal-info.html) (Japanese).

## Samples and design concepts

An authorized low-interaction or adaptive decoy that returns fake state ordinarily is not unauthorized access. The supplied component is not a complete authentication service. Article 7 may apply when a person impersonates another access administrator, or otherwise makes users believe they are that administrator, and publicly displays or emails information requesting that service's credentials.

For a privileged honeyword session, an access administrator routing fake matches into their own isolated environment ordinarily is not unauthorized access under the Act. This is an interpretation of the statute, not a verified court decision on this exact design. Maintain genuine MFA and never issue production authority on a fake match. Impersonating another service's administrator, or otherwise making users believe they are that administrator, and publicly displaying or emailing information requesting its credentials can implicate Article 7.

Creating or placing an Attention Deadlock or Goal Hijack document in an authorized environment is not automatically a violation of the Unauthorized Computer Access Act. Directing an AI to use another person's credentials or bypass third-party access controls without authority can implicate Articles 2 and 3 according to the actual conduct. Application of the Criminal Code's provisions on improper electronic instructions depends on the content, purpose and other facts; not every natural-language instruction is automatically prohibited. [Criminal Code, Article 168-2 as of October 30, 2026](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/140AC0000000045?occasion_date=20261030).


## 2026 amendments to the Act on the Protection of Personal Information

The amendment act was promulgated on July 17, 2026, but most of its provisions were not yet in force as of this review. Its supplementary provisions provide for commencement on a date set by Cabinet Order within two years of promulgation, with specified provisions taking effect after six months. The Personal Information Protection Commission is still preparing related Cabinet Orders, rules and guidelines. This guide's APPI discussion reflects provisions in force on the review date. Recheck the commencement dates and implementation rules as they are finalized. ## Authority, response and third-party distribution

Defensive intent does not itself grant authority over another system. An apparent attacker IP can belong to a relay or third party. Respond within your own authority: isolate your systems, revoke credentials, preserve evidence, and contact the service provider or police.

Canary requests may also send the source IP address, time and User-Agent to the token provider or hosting operator, where they may be recorded. Check collected fields, retention and storage regions, keep access to logs restricted, and do not put customer IDs or other identifying data in a URL. A source IP alone does not establish an attacker's identity. Whether request data is personal information or personal-related information depends on linkage and use. The Personal Information Protection Commission explains that device identifiers may be personal-related information even when they do not identify an individual by themselves ([General Guidelines](https://www.ppc.go.jp/personalinfo/legal/guidelines_tsusoku/)).

Article 4 concerns acquisition of another person's identification code for unauthorized-access use. Article 6 concerns storage of an **illegitimately acquired** code for unauthorized-access use. Incidental discovery or authorized defensive handling is not automatically illegal. Article 5 has an exception for legitimate business or other justifiable reasons. [National Police Agency commentary](https://www.npa.go.jp/bureau/cyber/pdf/1_kaisetsu.pdf).

Downloading a distributed file or opening a URL alone does not automatically establish unauthorized access; assess actual access controls, authority and actions. Third-party harm does not automatically establish the operator's civil liability; foreseeability, causation and other facts matter. Access logs and email addresses can qualify as personal information when individuals can be identified through readily available linkage; manage purpose, access and retention accordingly. See the [Personal Information Protection Commission FAQ](https://www.ppc.go.jp/all_faq_index/faq2-q2-2/) and its [general guidelines, revised June 2026](https://www.ppc.go.jp/personalinfo/legal/guidelines_tsusoku/).

Do not embed instructions intended to make a recipient's AI send third-party confidential information or personal data outside its authorized environment. Depending on what the publisher could foresee, intent or negligence, causation and the resulting harm, this may raise tort liability under Civil Code Article 709, contractual liability, or separate privacy-law issues. Publishing a file alone does not automatically establish liability, and the recipient's decision to submit it to an AI service does not automatically immunize the publisher. Organizations entering personal data into a cloud AI service should confirm that the input is necessary for the stated purpose and how the provider handles or uses that data. [Civil Code Article 709](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/129AC0000000089?occasion_date=20261030) / [PPC notice on generative AI services](https://www.ppc.go.jp/news/careful_information/230602_AI_utilize_alert/) / [PPC FAQ on personal data entered into cloud AI services](https://www.ppc.go.jp/all_fad_index/fad2-3-3-q1/) (Japanese).
